The vast majority of companies around the world now recognize that **ESG is a key indicator for evaluating corporate sustainability and social responsibility.** However, the practical challenge is that there remains a significant gap between recognizing the importance of ESG and **turning it into tangible results that can be demonstrated and verified.**

Particularly in the area of Social Impact, it is important to move beyond simple donations or one-time corporate social responsibility initiatives and establish **public-interest project models that can objectively measure and continuously track a company’s social contributions through quantitative metrics and data.**

Social value that cannot be measured is difficult to demonstrate objectively, and outcomes that cannot be objectively demonstrated are likely to remain limited to internal ESG reports or declarative statements. In contrast, if specific outcomes—such as the number of beneficiaries, jobs created, increases in local residents’ income, rates of self-reliance, and improvements in access to education and culture—can be quantified, these results can be visualized through data and charts and compared and verified on a year-by-year basis.

Therefore, what companies need is not simply **“a public-interest project that does good,”** but rather **“a public-interest project that creates social value, measures that value, and demonstrates it through data.”**

Only when such quantitative data is accumulated can a company’s ESG activities move beyond the level of internal reporting or corporate promotion and develop into objective performance data that can be used within international ESG evaluation and disclosure frameworks.

Ultimately, the key challenge of ESG is **not merely to create social value, but to establish a structure that can measure and demonstrate how much social value has actually been created.**

## Micro Cinemas Vision Statement — Films for Underserved Communities

When establishing an AI research lab, visibility is essential—but traditional marketing often feels transactional and wasteful. My goal is to redefine how promotional capital is used: instead of pouring budget into conventional advertising, we invest those funds into producing high-quality art films.

All proceeds from these films will go directly toward supporting children battling rare and incurable illnesses. By turning marketing spend into meaningful art and real-world relief, we build a self-sustaining cycle where cutting-edge AI research fuels artistic storytelling, and cinema directly enriches lives. It is marketing reimagined through public good—bringing recognition to our lab while making the world a genuinely warmer place.

Many Official Development Assistance (ODA) programs around the world have played an important role in alleviating poverty and inequality. However, a significant portion of such assistance still remains largely charitable in nature, relying heavily on external funding and external organizations for its operation. Under this structure, when ODA agencies or supporting organizations withdraw, the sustainability of these initiatives may weaken, potentially leaving local communities vulnerable once again.

Micro Cinema is designed to move beyond these limitations. Rather than simply providing resources or services, its core objective is to **build the capacity of local communities to operate independently, generate their own revenue, and sustain themselves over the long term.**

In other words, the purpose of Micro Cinema is not to provide one-time benefits to underserved communities, but to create a sustainable economic and cultural ecosystem in which local residents directly participate in and manage its operations. Even after external support ends, operations and revenue generation should continue within the community. Ultimately, Micro Cinema aims to facilitate a **transition from “communities that receive support” to “communities that are capable of growing on their own.”**

This **development of self-reliance and the establishment of a sustainable framework for growth** are closely aligned with the Social Impact dimension of ESG. Genuine social impact should not be measured solely by how much assistance has been provided, but by **whether that assistance leaves behind the structures and capabilities that enable communities to continue growing independently after external support has ended.**

Therefore, Micro Cinema is more than a cultural or social welfare initiative. It is a **sustainable Social Impact model that uses cultural content as a platform to simultaneously strengthen the economic self-reliance and social capacity of local communities.**

## Why ESG Should Pay Attention to Art Films

The core of ESG is not simply promoting a company. What matters is the lasting value that a company’s resources and activities create for society. From this perspective, art films can serve as an effective ESG tool that goes beyond one-time promotion and creates both cultural value and social impact.

The impact of conventional marketing often fades quickly once a campaign ends. Even if a company spends hundreds of thousands of dollars hiring a well-known brand ambassador and invests a multimillion-dollar advertising budget, the campaign may not remain in the public’s memory for long or lead to meaningful social value. Traditional marketing certainly has an important role to play, but simply spending money to increase brand awareness is not enough to create a sustainable virtuous cycle.

By contrast, an art film with strong artistic quality and social meaning can become a cultural asset that continues to be screened over time. It can be shown repeatedly at art-house cinemas, film festivals, and various cultural platforms around the world. In the process, audiences can naturally learn about the vision and work of the research institute. By introducing the institute at the beginning or end of the film, we can build brand awareness that is remembered together with the film’s message, rather than consumed as ordinary advertising.

More importantly, the revenue generated by the film can be connected to social support. All film proceeds received by the AI research institute can be used to support children suffering from rare and incurable diseases in the relevant country through partnerships with international organizations such as UNICEF, as well as local hospitals and public-interest institutions. In this way, the film becomes more than a promotional tool. It becomes a vehicle for creating social value—raising awareness of the research institute while delivering resources to people who genuinely need help.

The strength of art films lies in their sustainability. Unlike advertisements that are seen once and quickly disappear, films can continue to be screened and reach new audiences. Each screening can communicate the research institute’s vision and philosophy of the common good, while the revenue generated can once again be used to support children with rare and incurable diseases.

Ultimately, a single art film can create a virtuous cycle that introduces the institute’s technology and philosophy while generating cultural inspiration and social support.

This is why ESG should pay attention to art films. Art films connect marketing and social contribution as part of a single, sustainable social impact model, rather than treating them as separate activities. They raise awareness of the research institute, provide audiences with a meaningful experience, and contribute to the common good through the revenue generated by the film.

**A single film can introduce a research institute, move people’s hearts, and genuinely change the lives of children who need help.**

## The Expansion of the Common Good

Submitting a charity film that supports children with incurable diseases to European film festivals and screening it in art-house cinemas worldwide is far more than just showing a movie. By donating 100% of the proceeds returned to the AI research lab to UNICEF and partnering with local municipalities to host screenings at civic centers, we can deliver practical medical care and hope to children battling rare illnesses in those regions.

Furthermore, this project expands into an initiative to create "micro-cinemas" in villages across low-income countries by donating art films alongside compact screens and beam projectors. Instead of taking to the streets to beg for survival, children in these communities can build a foundation for self-reliance by sharing inspiring cinema with travelers from around the globe.

Using film as a means of charity, donation, and sustainable self-reliance—this is the true definition of the "Common Good," where cultural inspiration seamlessly unites with social impact.

## Turning Marketing Costs into Sustainable ESG Assets Through Micro Art Cinemas

Traditional marketing often loses its impact quickly once a campaign ends. Advertising spending can help increase corporate awareness, but in most cases, it does not accumulate into an asset that generates lasting revenue or leaves behind a foundation for local communities to become self-sustaining.

By contrast, a **Micro Art Cinema** can use the same resources to leave behind a sustainable cultural and economic foundation within a local community.

For example, imagine providing vulnerable residential areas or low-income village communities in the Philippines with a beam projector, a small screen and audio equipment, and at least five independently produced art films, all free of charge. There is no need to build a large movie theater. By using an existing village hall or shared community space, even a small space can be transformed into a **local cinema**.

The key is not simply to show films for free. It is about **creating a small cultural infrastructure that the local community can operate itself and use to generate revenue.**

Affordable admission fees could be charged to tourists and local residents, with the revenue managed by the community itself. The proceeds could then be used for areas the community genuinely needs, such as food, hygiene supplies, education, and facility maintenance and repairs. If the operation becomes stable, it could develop into a structure in which the community repeatedly generates modest amounts of income on its own, rather than depending solely on one-time external donations.

From a corporate perspective, this is also different from a simple donation.

**Advertising is an activity in which money is spent to gain people's attention, while a Micro Art Cinema is a model that transforms corporate resources into an asset that a local community can continue to use.**

More importantly, its impact can be measured. Annual attendance, local community participation rates, ticket revenue, operating costs, the amount reinvested into the community, the number of cultural programs held, and the number of people employed or involved in operations can all be continuously recorded. Therefore, rather than being an abstract promotional claim that a company has simply “done something good,” it can become **an ESG project whose real social impact can be tracked through data.**

Art films are also fundamentally different from advertising content that is used once and then disappears. Independently produced films can be screened repeatedly across multiple locations, while new works can be added over time to build a growing library of cultural content. Once a successful Micro Art Cinema model has been established, it can also be replicated in other low-income communities.

Ultimately, what matters is **not how much money was spent, but what that spending left behind.**

If $1 million is spent on a short-term global advertising campaign, its impact may decline once the campaign ends. However, if even a portion of those same resources is invested in establishing Micro Art Cinemas across multiple communities, the equipment, content, operational experience, and local revenue structure can remain in those communities even after the original project has ended.

The company creates cultural value, the local community gains a small economic foundation that it can operate itself, and audiences gain access to new cultural experiences.

**Instead of simply consuming a marketing budget, the goal is to transform it into a measurable ESG asset that local communities can operate, sustain, and grow themselves.**

This is the most important value of the Micro Art Cinema model.

**Donation → Cinema Installation → Admission Revenue → Community Reinvestment → Impact Measurement → Replication in Other Communities**

But if security is reduced to technical defense alone, we can lose sight of a more fundamental question: **What are we protecting, and whom are we protecting it for?**

To fulfill its purpose, security requires more than technical expertise. It requires a genuine commitment to values such as **human safety, freedom, dignity, and the Common Good**.

Ultimately, I believe security is not merely a technological issue. It is also a question of **what we choose to protect and why we choose to use technology to protect it**. Even the most advanced technology can become a tool for control or oppression if it is not guided by a commitment to the Common Good. Conversely, security technology grounded in that principle can serve as a genuine safeguard for people's safety and freedom.

The security I aspire to build is not merely security that stops attacks. It is **security that protects people—and security that exists in service of the Common Good**.

## If I Had a $1 Million Budget, I Would Like to Connect Art to the Sustainable Public Good

If I were given a budget of $1 million, rather than spending it on a one-time donation or promotional campaign, I would invest in building **a sustainable structure in which art itself can continuously create social value**.

First, I would like to propose external production partnerships with art and film institutions in France, such as **Le Fresnoy and La Fémis**. With a production budget of approximately $20,000 to $30,000 per film, I would produce around ten independent art films of a quality suitable for submission to various international film festivals. The important point would not simply be to make films, but to design these works from the beginning as **cultural assets with a social purpose**.

Especially if we could establish an external partnership with the prestigious La Fémis focused on the public good, I believe it could help foster and spread a culture of giving much more quickly.

Next, I would like to expand the public-good mission of our security research institute beyond technology. We could partner with local organizations in villages and impoverished communities across low-income countries around the world and provide them with the art films we produce, along with basic screening equipment such as small screens and projectors. Rather than building large movie theaters, the idea would be to create **small “Micro Cinemas” that local communities could operate themselves**.

These Micro Cinemas would be important because they would go beyond simple cultural donations. Through the media and social media, we could show where the films are being screened and how the revenue is being returned to the local communities. If both local residents and tourists visiting these areas could watch the films for an affordable admission fee of around $2 to $3, these small cinemas could gradually develop into a model that allows communities to generate income for themselves.

Why is the admission fee only $2 to $3? Because the goal is to make the cinema affordable and accessible, while building local word of mouth and giving tourists something enjoyable to experience. Additional income can come from selling locally sourced drinks and snacks for around $3 to $4, helping foster a spirit of self-reliance within the community.

When international tourists have a satisfying experience watching these films, word of mouth can spread and attract even more visitors, helping create a sustainable cycle that enables impoverished communities to build and maintain long-term self-reliance.

The production process, directors, and meaning behind each film could also be introduced through YouTube and social media. In particular, if we clearly communicate the social purpose that **“the proceeds from this film are used to support the self-reliance of impoverished communities and help children suffering from serious and rare diseases,”** audiences would not simply be consuming a film. By watching a film, they would also be participating in the public good.

I believe this represents an important possibility for art films. **A film does not end with a single donation.** It can be screened at film festivals in different countries, continue to be introduced online, and be shown repeatedly on small screens in local communities. Even as time passes, the work remains. It can continue to meet new audiences and create social value in the process.

Ultimately, what I want to create is neither simply a film production project nor simply a poverty-relief program. It is **a circular structure that gives artists opportunities to create their work, gives audiences opportunities to experience meaningful films, provides low-income communities with a small foundation for economic self-reliance, and uses part of the resulting revenue to help children with serious and rare diseases and other vulnerable people.**

**Art films do not necessarily have to achieve major commercial success. Even a small film can connect people for many years, and if the value it creates can then be used to help someone else, art itself can become a sustainable public good.**

I believe that art films can help impoverished communities become more self-reliant, support children suffering from serious and rare diseases, and at the same time provide opportunities for emerging artists. **Rather than spending $1 million once, I would use that $1 million to build a cultural ecosystem capable of creating social value for years to come.** That is how I would like to use this budget.
